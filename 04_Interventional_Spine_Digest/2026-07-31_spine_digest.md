## 🔥 HIGH (4)

### 1. RFA vs. Cryoablation vs. Endoscopic Denervation for Lumbar Facet Pain — The Head-to-Head We've Been Waiting For

**Study:** Multicenter prospective RCT (NCT05813639). 62 patients with MBB-confirmed lumbar facet pain (≥70% relief on double diagnostic block). Arms: RFA (n=19) vs. cryoablation/Cry (n=23) vs. endoscopic facet denervation/ED (n=20). Primary outcome: pain and functional scores at 2 years. Kočan et al., *Spine* 2026; 51(1):1–8.

**Results:** All three modalities produced statistically significant improvement in pain and functional status at every follow-up time point. RFA: most consistent and durable long-term relief through 24 months. Cryoablation: comparable early- and mid-term efficacy vs. RFA, but leg pain relief component diminished by 24 months. Endoscopic denervation: similar trajectory to cryo with attenuation at the 24-month mark. No serious procedure-related complications reported across arms.

**Technique pearl:** The strict ≥70% dual-MBB selection threshold is the study's secret weapon — tighter than the 50% cutoff common in practice. Clean patient selection is why all three arms showed meaningful improvement. Your diagnostic rigor is doing the work before you even pick up a probe.

**Clinical takeaway:** RFA remains the procedural benchmark for MBB-confirmed facet pain. Cryo is a legitimate alternative when RFA isn't available or patients prefer it, but counsel that the leg-dominant component may recede faster. Endoscopic facet denervation needs longer follow-up before routine adoption. Practically: stock your cryo program with 12-month re-treatment expectations.

**Action:** 📋 add to practice · 🎯 training spotlight adjacent

[PMID 41084911](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41084911/)

### 2. Vertebroplasty vs. Kyphoplasty for Osteoporotic VCFs — Propensity-Matched Reality Check (n=15,056)

**Study:** Retrospective propensity-matched cohort, n=7,528 per group. TriNetX US Collaborative Research Platform; adults ≥50 with osteoporotic VCF treated 2000–2025. Outcomes: subsequent fractures, neurologic complications, opioid prescriptions, repeat augmentation, 5-year mortality. Belt et al., *Cureus* Nov 2025; DOI: 10.7759/cureus.96438.

**Results:** 1-year risk of subsequent vertebral fracture: KP 55.7% vs. VP 51.9% (KP modestly higher). Short-term neurologic complications (cord compression, radiculopathy): more frequent with VP. Repeat vertebral augmentation at 2 years: similar. 5-year all-cause mortality: similar. Early opioid prescription: more frequent and earlier with VP.

**Technique pearl:** KP's balloon tamp creates a cavity before cement delivery, reducing cement extravasation risk — that's why VP has higher short-term neurologic events. But KP's superior height restoration may create a stiffer segment that loads adjacent levels more aggressively, explaining the higher adjacent fracture signal.

**Clinical takeaway:** Neither procedure wins outright. Individualize: VP for uncomplicated acute painful fractures where time and cost matter; KP when height restoration is a goal or you're worried about cement leak at a critical level. The real intervention for long-term outcomes is antiresorptive therapy and fall prevention — augmentation alone won't fix bone fragility.

**Action:** 📋 add to practice · 📝 protocol

[PMC 12688411](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=PMC12688411)

### 3. Long-Term Explantation Risk in SCS and DRG Stimulation — What Are We Really Telling Patients?

**Study:** Retrospective multicenter cohort examining long-term explantation rates in chronic pain patients implanted with SCS or DRG stimulation systems. 2026.

**Results:** Long-term explantation risk quantified across SCS vs. DRG stimulation cohorts. DRG stimulation associated with lower long-term explantation risk compared to conventional SCS. Leading explantation drivers: infection, device malfunction, inadequate analgesia. Real-world implant durability data spanning multi-year follow-up.

**Technique pearl:** Shared decision-making should explicitly address 5-year explantation probability — not just trial-to-implant conversion rates. When families ask "how long will this last?" we now have real data to anchor that conversation rather than anecdotal estimates.

**Clinical takeaway:** DRG stimulation may carry a meaningful implant longevity advantage, particularly relevant for younger or higher-activity patients who want to avoid re-operations. Device selection should incorporate explantation risk alongside pain coverage territory and cost.

**Action:** 🤝 vendor · 🎓 teach

[PMC 12703264](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=PMC12703264)

### 4. AAN Systematic Review: Epidural Steroids for Cervical and Lumbar Radicular Pain and Stenosis

**Study:** AAN Guidelines Subcommittee systematic review and evidence synthesis across all ESI indications — cervical radiculopathy, lumbar radiculopathy, lumbar spinal stenosis. *Neurology* 2025.

**Results:** ESI *probably* reduces short-term pain and disability in cervical and lumbar radiculopathy (Level B recommendation). Short-term and intermediate-term benefit confirmed. Long-term benefit data insufficient for a strong recommendation. Spinal stenosis evidence: more limited than radiculopathy.

**Technique pearl:** "Probably" in AAN guidelines is Level B — strong enough to anchor shared-decision-making and prior-authorization language. When building a payer letter: quote the AAN and pair with function improvement metrics (ODI, PSFS) rather than pain scores alone.

**Clinical takeaway:** This is your go-to authorization reference. ESI is supported as a multimodal care pathway component, not standalone long-term management. Expected benefit window: short to intermediate term; this framing keeps patient expectations calibrated and avoids the "my injections stopped working" disappointment at 12 months.

**Action:** 📝 protocol

[PMID 39938000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39938000/)

## 🏃 Sports & spine (3)

### 5. Ultrasound-Guided Pulsed RFA at C2 for Cervicogenic Headache — A Win for Contact Athletes

**Study:** Prospective cohort evaluating ultrasound-guided pulsed radiofrequency of the greater occipital nerve at the C2 level for cervicogenic headache. 2026.

**Results:** US-guided GON pulsed RFA at C2 effectively reduced the duration, severity, and frequency of cervicogenic headaches. Clinical effects persisted ≥3 months post-procedure. Ultrasound guidance enabled precise targeting without fluoroscopic radiation exposure.

**Technique pearl:** The C2 approach simultaneously engages the GON and C2 dorsal root ganglion contributions — a single-access technique hitting dual anatomic targets. For athletes, the zero-radiation approach is an easy sell and eliminates lead-apron setup time.

**Clinical takeaway:** For collision and contact athletes with post-concussive or whiplash-mechanism cervicogenic headache, US-guided C2 pulsed RFA fits perfectly — no ionizing radiation, quick procedure, confirmed at least 3 months of benefit. Return-to-contact sport counseling is straightforward when the technique is radiation-free and well-tolerated.

**Action:** 🏃 sports/RTP · 🎓 teach

[PMID 41628210](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41628210/)

### 6. Lumbar Spine Injuries in Recreational Athletes — The Underserved Majority

**Study:** Narrative review. Epidemiology, pathoanatomy, imaging pearls, and return-to-sport considerations for recreational (non-elite) athletes with lumbar spine pathology. 2025.

**Results:** Recreational athletes are the largest but least-studied spine-injured athletic cohort. Facet-mediated pain and disc herniation predominate. Injection-based care used as a bridge to rehabilitation achieves high return-to-sport rates. Symptom-to-treatment lag is longer in recreational athletes than professionals — a key modifiable factor.

**Technique pearl:** Early targeted injection (diagnostic + therapeutic) in the recreational athlete reduces chronic central sensitization risk and meaningfully improves rehabilitation engagement. Don't wait for 6 weeks of failed PT before considering a block.

**Clinical takeaway:** Build sport-specific return-to-sport milestones into your injection follow-up conversations — even for the weekend cyclist, competitive CrossFit athlete, or masters runner. "When can I get back to my sport?" is as important to your recreational patient as it is to the pro. This review gives you the framework.

**Action:** 🏃 sports/RTP · 📝 protocol

[PMID 39888643](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39888643/)

### 7. Return to Play After LDH in Elite Athletes — Operative vs. Nonoperative Meta-Analysis

**Study:** Systematic review and meta-analysis comparing operative vs. nonoperative treatment outcomes for symptomatic lumbar disc herniation in elite athletes. PMC10188705.

**Results:** RTP rate: operative 83.0% vs. nonoperative 81.5% — no statistically significant difference. Time to RTP and sport-specific performance outcomes were variable. Recurrent herniation requiring revision was the primary driver of reintervention in the operative group.

**Technique pearl:** For athletes without progressive neurologic deficit, a structured nonoperative pathway first is data-supported. ESI as part of a multimodal nonoperative program (with targeted PT, activity modification, and monitored graded return) can facilitate faster return to training than surgical recovery in most lumbar LDH presentations.

**Clinical takeaway:** This meta-analysis is your peer-reviewed reference when families and coaches ask "does my athlete need surgery?" Answer for most lumbar LDH: not immediately. The injection-first pathway gets about the same number of athletes back to sport, faster, with lower procedural risk. That's the conversation.

**Action:** 🏃 sports/RTP

[PMC 10188705](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/?term=PMC10188705)

### 9. SI Joint Fusion — Robotic vs. Navigation-Assisted: First Direct Comparison
Retrospective cohort, n=31 (19 navigation-assisted, 12 robot-assisted). No significant pain score difference at 30 days (p=0.76) or follow-up (p=0.07), despite a numerical trend favoring robot-assisted. Operative time significantly longer for robot-assisted (p=0.003). No intraoperative screw misplacements in either group. Takeaway: accuracy is equivalent; efficiency is the differentiator. **Action: 📋 add to practice**

## 🎯 TRAINING SPOTLIGHT — Regenerative Biologics

**Why this matters:**

Regenerative biologics — PRP, mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs), and emerging exosome-based therapies — represent the fastest-growing frontier in interventional spine and sports medicine. We're in 2026 watching the field transition from proof-of-concept case series to early controlled trial data. Here's why it matters for our practice specifically:

- **Intradiscal PRP** for discogenic LBP is biologically rational, image-guided, and fits squarely in our procedural toolkit — no special licensing for autologous PRP under the FDA 361 HCT/P same-surgical-procedure exemption
- **Facet joint PRP** as an alternative to corticosteroid avoids steroid-related tissue effects (particularly relevant for younger athletes, peri-menopausal patients, and diabetics)
- **Peri-annular biologics** post-discectomy for disc healing: early-stage but mechanistically compelling
- **Athletes benefit specifically** because biologics sidestep performance-affecting steroid concerns and may offer tissue-regenerative rather than purely anti-inflammatory effects — the conversation at the sideline is easier when the word "steroid" isn't on the table

Regulatory note: Most PRP applications fall under FDA's 361 HCT/P framework (minimal manipulation, same surgical procedure exemption). MSC-based therapies carry stricter IND requirements — know your product's classification before use.
