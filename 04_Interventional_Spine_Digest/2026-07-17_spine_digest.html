🔥 HIGH (5)

HIGH I10 Discography

1. Discography does NOT increase reoperation risk at 10–20 years

Ohnmeiss DD, Courtois EC, Guyer RD, Blumenthal SL, Shellock JL, Zigler JE
Spine 51(13):969–973, July 1, 2026  |  PMID 41292254

Study: Retrospective matched cohort. 196 patients, 244 discs with discography vs. 122 discs without. Minimum 10-year F/U (mean 152.9 months, range 120–247).

Results: Reoperation rate for disc-related pain: 10.2% (discography) vs. 8.3% (control), P>0.50 — no significant difference across lumbar levels.

Technique pearl: This reassurance applies to pressure-controlled, low-volume discography conforming to SIS/IASP standards. Don't conflate with older high-pressure paradigms.

Clinical takeaway: The strongest long-term data yet that properly performed discography does not increase reoperation risk over two decades. This should finally settle the debate haunting your consent forms.

HIGH I02+I15 RFA/Cryo/Endo

2. Three-arm multicenter RCT: RFA vs. Cryoablation vs. Endoscopic Denervation for lumbar facet pain

EuroPainClinics® Network (Slovakia) — 3 centers
Spine 51(1), January 2026  |  PMID 41084911

Study: Prospective, multicenter, randomized, parallel-group. 62 patients, ≥70% relief on dual diagnostic MBBs. RFA n=19, Cryo n=23, Endo n=20. F/U at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months (NRS, ODI).

Results: All 3 groups: significant, sustained improvement at 24 months. RFA = most consistent long-term. Cryo = comparable at 6–12 months; leg pain benefit waned at 24 months. Endo = fastest early back pain response; less durable for referred leg pain. No significant between-group differences on NRS or ODI at any timepoint.

Technique pearl: Endoscopic denervation targets the capsule and periarticular structures, not just the medial branch — potentially different indications than pure neurolytic approaches. Consider for prior RFA failures with imaging showing significant synovitis.

Clinical takeaway: You can't go wrong with any of these three for properly selected facet pain. But if you want durability data, RFA still leads. Cryo is compelling for patients who prefer the reversibility argument. Endo is promising but leg-pain durability data is lacking.

HIGH I15 Cryo

3. Cryo beats RFA at 12 months in lumbar medial branch pilot RCT

Ferrillo MG, Brady OD et al.
Pain Physician 28(6):E677–E687, November 2025  |  PMID 41337771

Study: Single-blind, randomized pilot RCT (NCT06016127). 30 patients, 15/arm. Lumbar medial branch cryoneurolysis vs. conventional RFA. Primary outcomes: NRS and function at Day 180 and Day 360.

Results: Cryoneurolysis: significantly lower pain scores at Day 180 and Day 360 vs. RFA. Both groups improved substantially; cryo edge persistent and statistically significant. No major adverse events in either arm.

Technique pearl: Cryo probes require precise placement to achieve adequate freeze zone at nerve contact. Fluoroscopic or ultrasound confirmation of probe-to-nerve distance is critical. Nerve regrows (Wallerian degeneration → regeneration): reversible, but may require re-treatment at ~6–9 months for some patients.

Clinical takeaway: This pilot, combined with the 3-arm RCT above, builds a compelling case that cryo is at minimum non-inferior and possibly superior to conventional RFA at 12 months. If you haven't tried cryo, now's the time to get hands-on.

HIGH I13 Sports/RTP

4. 🏃 Expert consensus: return-to-sport after cervical spine surgery in athletes

Modified Delphi Consensus Panel
Spine, 2026  |  PMID 40838273

Study: Modified Delphi survey. Addressed RTS after cervical surgery across multiple pathologies (herniation, stenosis, instability, ACDF, disc replacement) and sport types (high-impact, high-frequency).

Results: Consensus achieved for RTS after surgical treatment across all cervical pathologies studied. Athletes must be pain-free, neurologically intact, and at full cervical strength/ROM before clearance — with formal specialist endorsement now in place across injury types.

Technique pearl: Pre-clearance: dynamic flex/ext imaging after fusion to confirm hardware integrity; sport-specific load testing (axial compression tolerance, rotation) before full contact return.

Clinical takeaway: This is the paper for your sports-spine clinic folder. When a high-school football player or wrestler pushes for clearance 3 months post-ACDF, you now have peer-reviewed, expert-consensus backing for your decision — in both directions.

HIGH I02 RFA

5. RFA outperforms steroid injection for lumbar facet pain: 132-patient prospective study

Published June 2026
Annals of African Medicine — DOI: 10.4103/aam.aam_12_26  |  PMID 42377934

Study: Prospective comparative study, 132 adults with lumbar facet pain confirmed by MBB. RFA vs. corticosteroid injection.

Results: RFA: superior long-term pain relief and functional improvement. Duration of relief favored RFA. Disability scores also favored RFA. No major complications.

Technique pearl: Selection by confirmatory MBB is the key differentiator. The MBB responders are the ones who benefit most from RFA — don't skip the diagnostic step.

Clinical takeaway: Clean, reasonably large prospective study reinforcing what we already practice. Excellent for payers still pushing steroid injection as preferred first-line therapy for facet pain.

🎯 TRAINING SPOTLIGHT — Endoscopic Spine

Why this matters right now

Endoscopic spine is entering mainstream US practice. Uniportal and biportal endoscopic approaches now achieve full lumbar decompression, discectomy, and interbody fusion through 8–10 mm portals — blood loss often <50 mL, same-day discharge, outcomes equivalent or superior to open surgery in controlled trials. This week's multicenter RCT data (PMID 41084911) also shows endoscopic denervation is entering the facet pain space alongside RFA and cryo. Whether you're an interventionalist, physiatrist, or pain physician, understanding which patients are candidates and being able to refer intelligently is now a core competency.

Training pathway

  • NASS (spine.org): Annual meeting cadaver workshops; NASS Education portal — check for full-endoscopic and biportal hands-on courses.
  • SIS (sis.org): Spine Intervention Society; minimally invasive spine training with evolving endoscopic applications in the interventional context.
  • AAPM&R (aapmr.org): Annual meeting — interventional spine track periodically includes endoscopic approaches for physiatrists bridging to procedural work.

Always verify current course availability directly with the listed organization — curricula update annually.

High-yield reading

PMC12985621 — "Biportal Endoscopic Spine Surgery: Evolution of Techniques, Indications, and Influential Literature" (J Clin Med, Feb 2026) — best single-paper orientation to where the field has been and where it's going.
Also: PMID 41084911 (3-arm RCT) for the cryo/endo/RFA intersection context.

Practical first step

  1. Identify your highest-volume endoscopic spine surgeon locally — request a proctored OR observation for a 1-level lumbar decompression case.
  2. Review NASS/SIS course schedules for the next 6 months; register for a wet lab before spots fill.
  3. For the interventionalist: start tracking which MILD/ESI patients might be better served by endoscopic decompression — it sharpens your referral pattern immediately.
