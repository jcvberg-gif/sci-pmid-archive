### 1. Discography does NOT increase reoperation risk at 10–20 years
**Ohnmeiss DD, Courtois EC, Guyer RD, Blumenthal SL, Shellock JL, Zigler JE**
*Spine* 51(13):969–973, July 1, 2026
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41292254/

**Study:** Retrospective matched cohort. 196 patients, 244 discs with discography vs. 122 discs without. Minimum 10-year F/U (mean 152.9 months, range 120–247).

**Results:** Reoperation rate for disc-related pain: **10.2% (discography) vs. 8.3% (control), P>0.50** — no significant difference. This held across lumbar levels. Complication rate from discography not elevated.

**Technique pearl:** The reassurance here applies to pressure-controlled discography conforming to SIS/IASP standards — low-pressure, low-volume technique matters. Don't conflate with older high-pressure provocation paradigms.

**Clinical takeaway:** When patients (or attorneys) ask whether the discogram "damaged their disc," you now have the strongest long-term data yet that properly performed discography does not increase reoperation risk over two decades. This should finally settle the debate that's haunted our consent forms.

**Action tags:** 📝 protocol update · 🎓 teach residents

### 2. Three-arm multicenter RCT: RFA vs. Cryoablation vs. Endoscopic Denervation for lumbar facet pain
**EuroPainClinics® Network (Slovakia)**
*Spine* 51(1), January 2026
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41084911/

**Study:** Prospective, multicenter, randomized, parallel-group trial. 62 patients, chronic lumbar facet syndrome confirmed with ≥70% relief on dual diagnostic MBBs. RFA n=19, Cryo n=23, Endoscopic n=20. Outcomes at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months (NRS, ODI).

**Results:** All 3 groups showed **significant and sustained improvement** in back pain and disability at 24 months. RFA = most consistent long-term relief. Cryo = comparable at 6 and 12 months but leg-pain benefit diminished at 24 months. Endoscopic = fastest early back pain response, less durable for referred leg pain. No statistically significant between-group differences on NRS or ODI at any timepoint.

**Technique pearl:** Endoscopic denervation targets the capsule and periarticular structures, not just the medial branch — it may have different indications than pure neurolytic approaches. Best for patients who've had prior RFA failures or imaging showing significant synovitis.

**Clinical takeaway:** You can't go wrong with any of these three modalities for properly selected facet pain — but if you want the most durable data, RFA still leads. Cryo is a compelling alternative for patients who want the reversibility pitch. Endo is emerging but lacks the 2-year leg pain data.

**Action tags:** 📝 protocol · 🤝 vendor conversation on cryo/endo equipment · 🎯 training spotlight tie-in

### 3. Cryo beats RFA at 12 months in lumbar medial branch pilot RCT
**Ferrillo MG, Brady OD et al.**
*Pain Physician* 28(6):E677–E687, November 2025
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41337771/

**Study:** Single-blind, randomized pilot RCT (NCT06016127). 30 patients, 15 per arm, lumbar medial branch cryoneurolysis vs. conventional RFA. Primary outcomes: NRS and functional improvement at Day 180 and Day 360.

**Results:** Cryoneurolysis group had **significantly lower pain scores** at both Day 180 and Day 360 vs. RFA (exact deltas pending full text — pilot-level precision). Both groups improved; cryo edge was persistent and statistically significant at both timepoints. No major adverse events in either group.

**Technique pearl:** Cryo probes require more precise placement to achieve adequate freeze zone over the nerve — fluoroscopic or ultrasound confirmation of probe-to-nerve contact distance is critical. The nerve regrows (Wallerian degeneration → regeneration), which explains the reversibility but also means effects are time-limited (~6–9 months before re-treatment is needed for some patients).

**Clinical takeaway:** This pilot, combined with the 3-arm RCT above, makes a compelling case that cryo is at minimum non-inferior and possibly superior to conventional RFA for lumbar facet pain at 12 months. Don't throw away your RFA cannulas — but if you haven't tried cryo, now's the time to get trained.

**Action tags:** 📝 protocol · 🎯 training spotlight · 🤝 discuss cryoneurolysis device options

### 4. 🏃 Expert consensus on return-to-sport after cervical spine surgery in athletes
**Modified Delphi Consensus Panel**
*Spine*, 2026
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40838273/

**Study:** Modified Delphi consensus survey of subspecialty experts. Addressed return-to-high-impact/high-frequency sport after cervical surgery across a range of pathologies (herniation, stenosis, instability, ACDF, disc replacement).

**Results:** Consensus achieved to recommend return-to-sport after surgical treatment of multiple cervical pathologies in athletes. Specific timelines codified by pathology and surgical approach. The panel recommends athletes be **pain-free, neurologically intact, and at full cervical strength/ROM** before clearance — consistent with prior guidance — but now with formal specialist endorsement across injury types.

**Technique pearl:** Pre-clearance evaluation should include dynamic flexion-extension imaging after fusion to confirm hardware integrity, and sport-specific load testing (axial compression tolerance, rotation) before full contact return.

**Clinical takeaway:** This is the paper to put in your sports-spine clinic folder. When a high-school football player or college wrestler is 3 months out from ACDF and pushing for clearance, you now have peer-reviewed, expert-consensus backing for your decision. Print it.

**Action tags:** 🏃 sports/RTP · 📝 clinic protocol · 🎓 teach

### 5. RFA outperforms steroid injection for lumbar facet pain: prospective study, 132 patients
**Annals of African Medicine, June 2026**
*DOI: 10.4103/aam.aam_12_26*
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42377934/

**Study:** Prospective comparative study, 132 adult patients with lumbar facet pain, confirmatory medial branch block, RFA vs. corticosteroid injection.

**Results:** RFA provides **superior long-term pain relief and functional improvement** vs. steroid injection for facet-mediated pain. Duration of relief longer in RFA arm. Functional outcomes (disability scores) also favored RFA. No major complications reported.

**Technique pearl:** Selection criterion matters — all patients had confirmatory MBB. Don't jump to RFA without proper diagnostic block first; the MBB responders are the ones who benefit most from RFA.

**Clinical takeaway:** Not a new concept, but a clean, reasonably large prospective study reinforcing what we already practice. Good paper to show payers who still push steroid injection as the first-line preferred option for facet pain.

**Action tags:** 📝 prior auth ammunition · 📋 add to practice evidence library

**Clinical takeaway:** NFL-level data is our best surrogate for high-demand athletes. Brings hard numbers — games played, performance scores — to a conversation that's usually anecdotal. Useful framing for the elite-athlete family meeting.

### S3. Lumbar spine injuries in recreational athletes: a review
**PMID 39888643**
*Published 2025*

**Summary:** Reviewed lumbar disc herniation, spondylolysis, and disc degeneration in recreational athletes. Conservative management outcomes excellent. Microdiscectomy: RTS average 5–6 months. Lumbar fusion: RTS often >8–12 months and poses greater sport-specific challenges.

**Clinical takeaway:** For the weekend warrior who asks "when can I ski/CrossFit/cycle again," this gives you the evidence-based conversation scaffold by injury type. Conservative first, set realistic timelines, set the fusion expectations early.

**Action tag:** 🏃 sports/RTP · 🎓 teach

## 🎯 TRAINING SPOTLIGHT — Endoscopic Spine

**Why this matters to you right now:**
Endoscopic spine is no longer a niche technique from Korean fellowships — it's entering mainstream US practice. Uniportal and biportal endoscopic approaches now achieve full lumbar decompression, discectomy, and interbody fusion through 8–10 mm portals, with blood loss often <50 mL, same-day discharge, and outcomes equivalent or superior to open surgery in controlled trials. The multicenter data this week (PMC12685308, PMC12985621) underscores that endoscopic denervation is also being studied as an alternative to thermal RFA. Whether you're an interventionalist, a physiatrist, or a pain physician, understanding which of your patients are candidates — and being able to refer intelligently and counsel on recovery — is now a core competency.

**Training pathway:**
- **NASS (spine.org):** Annual meeting includes cadaver and hands-on endoscopic spine workshops; check the NASS Education portal for upcoming courses in full-endoscopic and biportal techniques.
- **SIS (sis.org):** Spine Intervention Society; minimally invasive spine training, including evolving endoscopic applications in the interventional pain context.
- **AAPM&R (aapmr.org):** Academy annual meeting; interventional spine track sessions periodically include emerging endoscopic approaches, particularly for physiatrists bridging to procedural work.

*Note: Always verify current course availability directly with the listed organization — curricula update annually.*

**High-yield reading paper:**
PMC12985621 — "Biportal Endoscopic Spine Surgery: Evolution of Techniques, Indications, and Influential Literature" (J Clin Med, Feb 2026) is the best single-paper orientation to where the field has been and where it's going.
Also: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41084911/ (3-arm RCT) for the cryo/endo/RFA intersection.

**Practical first step:**
1. Identify your highest-volume endoscopic spine surgeon locally and request a proctored OR observation for a 1-level lumbar decompression.
2. Review the NASS/SIS course schedule for the next 6 months and register for a wet lab before spots fill.
3. For the interventionalist: start tracking which of your MILD/ESI patients might be better served by endoscopic decompression — it sharpens your referral pattern.
